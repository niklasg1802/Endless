# THE MAYO POST-MORTEM AND THE WRITERS' ROOM SPEC

## 1. WHY OUR SCRIPT FAILED

Brutal and mapped:

1. **There is no premise. There is a location.** "A world made of mayonnaise" is a setting. A Rick and Morty premise is one mundane domestic commitment obeyed with total internal logic until it reaches catastrophic scale ("what if he turned himself into a pickle" -> then, "why would he?"). Mayo is a noun. Nobody in our episode ever asks *why*. Fails RULE 4 and the corrected premise test: *whose fault is this, and why would they choose it?* Answer in our script: nobody's, and nobody.

2. **Nothing is anyone's fault.** Our plot is generated by the world, not by a person. Milk rises on its own. A vat tips. Pipes burst. The ray gun fires by itself. Harmon's actual loop is hook first, *then* "why would he do that?" — and the answer ("he's his own worst enemy, he'd want to prove himself") is what pulls the whole story into existence. We skipped the loop entirely, so we got weather, not story.

3. **The protagonist is a shrug.** The boy gets mayo'd and says "...okay." Flat acceptance is not a "You" step, it is the *absence* of a character. Harmon's camera is the kid's mind and Rick's mind is a black box the audience watches from outside; a kid with no want, no fear and no opinion has no mind to be behind. He is present to punctuate. (Verification killed the "conscience function" framing but the core holds: Morty is the naive anchor *who is changed by what he sees*. Ours sees everything and is changed by nothing.)

4. **The scientist is flippant with nothing underneath.** "I fixed the thing that makes things not be here" is a joke-shaped sentence with no mechanism behind it — no over-specific gibberish, no cold correct answer the room couldn't have predicted. Rick's contempt is funny because it is *correct* and unbearable at once. Ours is unbearable and incorrect.

5. **Our own best line is our worst crime.** "Everything I make works. That's the whole problem." is the thesis, delivered aloud, mid-catastrophe, by the character who should never be allowed to say it. Note the verification correction too: the Take is not *missing*, it's *malformed*. His trait (mastery that works) causes his disaster, but the disaster arrives as volume (a vat erupts) rather than as a bill for a choice. A thesis is what a character says when the story did not make the point.

6. **Every cut is obvious because every beat has one job.** A cut reads as "and then" when the transition is sequence and not consequence. Our beats were arrived at independently and welded with connectors. A cut becomes invisible when the transition is "because of what someone just did."

7. **No B-plot.** The laundry line is a scene, not a circle. Note the verification: the doctrine "two same-theme circles per episode" is overclaimed; the show's documented formula is a *tonal pairing* — gonzo sci-fi A-plot cut against a sincere domestic register, often with the flaw-loaded thread handed to the family. Either way we have neither: the domestic line is a soft landing after the adventure instead of the wound *under* it.

8. **The sincere beat stands alone.** Sincerity is a floor, not a room. One warm line, spotlighted, after the action = a drama with jokes bolted on.

9. **The tag has no debt.** "Gun fires by itself, chair vanishes" is a continuity nod, not a callback. A chair is a prop; nothing was spent, so the reversal costs nothing.

10. **No rules to break.** No time pressure, no hard rule, two portals, a world with no physics worth exploiting. A world with no rules is a world with no stakes.

**The one-line verdict:** we built the furniture and left out the engine. And the verification pass is right about where the engine actually lives: **a want/need mismatch, dramatised through a self-contained A/B comic scene pair, with consequence as punctuation — not as the point.**

## 2. THE ENGINE RULES

1. **THE PREMISE IS A SENTENCE, NOT A SET.** Write the domestic truth first with zero genre words on an index card. Then the machine's literal physical behavior must *be that sentence*. Machine came first? Throw it out. (Confidence: high; this is the strongest surviving finding.)
2. **THE WHY-LOOP IS MANDATORY.** Hook first, then ask "why would he do this to himself?" and make the first answer *character* — not plot. Rick exists as a premise generator because the answer is always "because he's his own worst enemy and he wants to prove it." (High.)
3. **THE LOOP IS SECOND.** The conceit is the entry point; the feeling usually gets *retrofitted* through the family. Do not demand the metaphor be encoded at pitch time — demand that by the end of the outline you can answer why these specific people did this to each other. (High — this corrects our first instinct.)
4. **ONE COMMITTED, ESCALATING ABSURDITY.** Commit to the dumbest version and obey it with total internal logic. Our failure is *Part 1*: milk rising and a vanishing lawn chair are mild and arbitrary. Escalate the idea until it hurts. (Medium-high.)
5. **A/B IS A PAIRING, NOT A MIRROR-IMAGE REQUIREMENT.** Do not hunt for two same-theme circles — the "two same-theme circles" doctrine is refuted. The working shape: gonzo A-plot driven by the flaw, sincere/domestic B-plot in the opposite register, converging at least twice — once so the domestic problem *causes* a cosmic problem, once so it's what *survives*. (High.)
6. **FLOW: WANT -> NEED.** Rick wants the wrong thing; the episode is the price of wanting it. Mismatched want/need is the house engine; consequence is punctuation. (High.)
7. **BREAK THE CIRCLE AFTER FIND, NOT AFTER TAKE.** Harmon's circle is scaffolding and the show routinely snaps it after step 5. Do not push the script toward moralising melodrama. (High.)
8. **THE MUNDANE-INTERRUPT.** At least once per scene, and always at the climax, a domestic objection outranks the crisis: laundry, homework, who's paying for the door. The joke is the *outranking*. (High.)
9. **ENDING CONTRACT: CLEAN WORLD, DIRTIER PROTAGONIST.** Everything returns to normal and it is worse. No catharsis. (High.)
10. **NO LESSON LEARNED.** If a character states the moral, take it away from them or reveal it as a lie. Stating the moral and ignoring it is fine; stating it and being *rewarded* is the thing to never do. Note: the strong version ("learning is always punished") is refuted — "Fear No Mort" and "Rixty Minutes" both reward insight. So the real law is: **no sentimental reformation, and it is withheld most reliably from Rick.** Morty may grow; Rick may not. (High.)
11. **THE TAKE MUST BE EARNED OR IT IS VOLUME.** A malformed Take (trait causes disaster, arriving as incident) reads as a hazard. Make the catastrophe the *bill for a specific choice* the character made on-screen. (High.)
12. **SOURCES ARE SOMEONE'S FAULT.** If the disaster has no author, you have written a horror movie. (High.)

## 3. JOKE RULES

1. **THE JOKE RATE.** No documented per-minute figure exists and you must not assert one. Practical target from structure: 2-4 real laugh opportunities per 30 seconds, and **at least one laugh attempt per shot** — with the hard rule that no shot is *only* a joke (see #2). The sharpest possible version: 35%-45% of usable runtime is comedy-forward, and at least one laugh attempt per shot. (Medium confidence — inferred.)
2. **THE BIT HAS THREE STAGES: PLANT / STRESS / PAY.** Establish the rule plainly, restate it under one new pressure, break it at the worst moment so the rule *becomes* the catastrophe. A one-liner hits stage (a) and stops. Our laundry line, ray gun, and vat are all stage (a).
3. **DEADPAN AGAINST IMAGE.** The line stays calm and boring; the picture does not. Vigorously true, and it's the same rule as the mundane-interrupt. (High.)
4. **THE UNCHANGED SENTENCE AGAINST CHANGED CONSEQUENCES.** "Twenty minutes" is structurally the right early seed, midsection recall (as *wrong*), and final receipt (as *correct, to the second*, which is the punchline). Tag-echoing a catchphrase is Comic Book Guy logic; repricing it is Rick logic.
5. **NO STANDALONE GAGS.** Every object planted must matter later. Interchangeable gadgets between acts = no continuity = no stakes. (High.)
6. **CAPS:** an established bit does not get to repeat on its own merits. The *mall pretzel* is structural, not a running gag. Do not let the show's own catchphrase survive past the handoff.

### Five techniques, each with an original example (MINE, not the show's):

**T1 — OVER-SPECIFIC GIBSH, CONFIDENT AND UNEXPLAINED.**
> "I've decoupled the ice from the water. The molecules are still there. They just don't like each other anymore." *(mine)*

**T2 — FLAT COSMIC SHRUG, LANDED ON TRIVIA.**
> "The universe is a corpse and we are the maggots. Anyway, the toaster's doing the thing again." *(mine)*

**T3 — SAME FACT, OPPOSITE SHAPE (abridge vs inflate).**
> RICK: "It's a vat." *(mine)*
> MORTY: "So the — so the thing is, it's like — it's a *vat*, and vats tip, and this one is *big*, and I'm the guy standing next to it, so—" *(mine)*

**T4 — THE APOLOGY AS A RESTAURANT REVIEW.**
> "I'm sorry. I'm not — I'm not good at sorry. It always comes out like a *review*. 'Two stars, would abandon again, the crying was uneven.'" *(mine)*

**T5 — THE MORAL MISTAKEN FOR A PREFERENCE.**
> "Staring at a pretend Mall for nine hours didn't *feel* like nothing to me. It felt like — like a whole *Sunday*." *(mine)*

## 4. CHARACTER ENGINE

**RICK**
- Core want: to matter, and to never be told what to do.
- Core fear: being ordinary, dependent, boring — being Jerry.
- Comic function: the de-escalator with a nuclear option. Delivers cosmic catastrophe like a chore. Deflates emotion with a bored technical correction, then has the power to end any sincere moment.
- Argues: never from feeling — always a smug factual correction that makes the other person feel stupid. He wins by making you feel dumb.
- Blind to: that his intelligence is a defence against grief, not a virtue.
- **Implementable rule:** his contempt must always be *correct*. Smugness is only earned if the physics is right.

**MORTY**
- Core want: to be liked, to be safe, to not have to decide.
- Core fear: that he's a coward and the people he loves will confirm it.
- Comic function: the naive anchor you occupy. Narrates an ordinary scene, then is forced to become a monster for two seconds.
- Argues: from how he feels right now — and flips if the room turns.
- Blind to: that doing "the right thing" is a gift he gives himself and costs other people everything.
- **Implemented engine:** right feeling + wrong method + paid price.

**JERRY**
- Core want: contentment, a quiet life, to be loved for not being special.
- Core fear: that he really is a loser.
- Comic function: the reality anchor that reverses scale.
- Argues: passive voice, "well actually" stats, then retreats into hurt.

**BETH**
- Core want: to be picked by her father, to be exceptional in his eyes.
- Core fear: that she is ordinary — that she settled.
- Comic function: the childhood sociopath grown into the suburban professional.
- Argues: like Rick — from validity ("you're a bad parent"), then straight to the throat.

**SUMMER**
- Core want: to be taken seriously and included.
- Core fear: being the disposable one whose whole dimension gets left behind.
- Comic function: reframes a cosmic problem as a status problem and is *right*.
- Argues: from social read and leverage; won't be shamed for wanting to be liked.

### Pairing dynamics that generate a scene
- **Rick vs Jerry** — cosmic contempt vs dim contentment. Neither can picture the other as real. Best used for *no-stakes* comedy with a hidden knife: Jerry genuinely doesn't get it, and Jerry is genuinely the only one not miserable.
- **Rick vs Morty** — infinite competence observed by finite panic. The camera is Morty's; Rick's mind is a black box the audience watches from outside. Rick produces a cold, correct answer to a problem we never saw him work; Morty supplies the commentary we think in.
- **Rick vs Rick** — the only person Rick can be *accurate* with is himself, which is why the scene reads as honesty even while the room reads it as fighting. Structural bonus: two Ricks makes the cut invisible, because the transition is "because of what one of him just did" — and the tag can be *himself* choosing the pretzel, no dialogue.
- **Never** build Rick/Summer on physical menace. Build it on Rick refusing to acknowledge competence in a register he considers beneath the annoyance threshold — and on the fact that his contempt is a hedge, not a credo. Leave the "he doesn't remember how many times someone was replaced" note unspoken; it's true, and it's the flinch I own.

## 5. DIALOGUE RULES

1. **IMPULSE OVER INFORMATION.** Name the impulse in one word before writing the line: *to be right / to escape / to shut them up / to get to the point / to avoid the subject.* If you can't name it, delete the line.
2. **NEVER LET EVERY LINE FINISH.** At least one line per exchange dies mid-word. Turn-based dialogue (A completes, B completes, A completes) is the signature of a bad script. This alone fixes most "obvious cut" feeling.
3. **THE TECHNICAL LINE IS 60% EXPLANATION, 40% CONTEMPT.** Start the explanation, get bored, insult the listener, let the plot information land as a tossed-off afterthought. Never deliver a clean quotable finished technical sentence.
4. **STAMMER IS CONTENT.** Every restart reveals mental state. A tangent that teaches us nothing about the speaker is filler. The derail *is* the character.
5. **SAME FACT, OPPOSITE SHAPE.** Rick abridges (3-8 words, contemptuous). Morty inflates (a re-explaining run-on with at least two restarts). Never let them match length or rhythm.
6. **VOICE-DIRECT SPEC per line:** IMPULSE, TARGET (who it's aimed at even if off-screen), SPEED (fast/normal/slur), DAMAGE (what it costs the speaker to say it). Direct for interrupt-timing and volume-*withdrawal*. Note: the burp and stammer mechanic is popular observation, not a cited creator interview — treat as inferred. Write interrupts as blank-verse *enjambment* (the verb lands on the next line's beat) rather than a bracketed `[INTERRUPT]` primitive, because a TTS engine reads brackets aloud.
7. **THE SINCERE-LINE RULE.** One line, inside a mundane action. Every other character stays busy with something practical. No brackets, no beat, no pause-for-effect, no acknowledgement from anyone in the scene. The audience supplies the gravity; the scene must not. If two sincere lines are adjacent, you wrote a drama.
8. **RESOLVE BY GLOSS.** Resolve a big sci-fi beat with one mundane sentence or object, never with more sci-fi.

## 6. COLD OPEN / ACT BREAK / TAG RULES

**COLD OPEN** — a complete micro-story with its own want and turn; plants one RULE, FLAW or OBJECT the plot will detonate. Not a mood, not a trailer — a loaded gun. It must also demonstrate the dual register (domestic mundane + cosmic extreme in the same breath). Ours was a standalone gag with one flat register.

**ACT BREAK** — a REVERSAL OF UNDERSTANDING, not a physical cliffhanger. A new fact that changes what the story is about: a lie exposed, a world worse than believed, the hero revealed as the cause. Danger is an obstacle, not a turn. Our act break was "the mayo gets wetter."

**TAG** — a CONSEQUENCE nobody dealt with, or a callback that re-opens the wound. It must *escalate* or *reframe*, never repeat. And it must cost someone something. The strongest form is silent: hold on the image long enough to land, no joke attached, no dialogue. Ours repeated a prop gag with zero stake.

**FULL STRUCTURE:** cold open (1-3 shots) / act one through the act break / act two / short return / tag. Break the circle after Find.

## 7. WORKED OUTLINE — "THE RICK THAT GOT AWAY"

### PREMISE CARD (written first, no genre words)
**The sentence:** *"I did everything for this family, and no one can even tell me whether it worked."*
Second card: *"I would rather be replaced than thanked."*
Third card (the flaw): *"I've been so many people for you, I don't know which one of me is the one you were talking to."*

**The step we're deliberately refusing:** any bar-napkin math about dog lifetimes, and any line counting how many times someone has been replaced. For a 3-minute cut, one axis is enough: *the only problem I can't fix is which me fixed it.*

**Why this world and not a mayonnaise world:** the machine is *Rick's own repair of a marriage in parallel* — the hinge is not a world, it is a possessive: **which me?** The absurdity escalates (one pretzel on eight receipts) until it hurts, and the C-route stinger is the same ask, unpaid, from someone who won't say the word "family."

### A / B / C
- **A-plot (Rick, gonzo sci-fi, flaw-driven):** Rick is hedging a fight with his wife by running every possible version of the move under the cover of parallel-extraction. The flaw is his: he would rather be replaced than thanked. Disaster is *authored* — his own relocation order gets re-processed by the clerk he browbeat.
- **B-plot (Morty, sincere/domestic, opposite register):** a kid whose value has been *calibrated* to the contract he was extracted under. His want is to be the one who "signed," to be needed by name. He has no plot; he has a floor, and it breaks.
- **C-plot / engine (Morty-as-Rick):** a version of Rick who has been extracted *to cover a fight but wasn't told which fight,* and who is left on the sidewalk holding the wrong question. This is the show's doubled-engine: our hero's cost is **unwitnessed needing**, and the tag hands the same unmet ask to the last person who would ever say it out loud.
- **Convergence 1:** the domestic problem *causes* the cosmic problem — Rick's attempt to find a version of himself who handles the fight well is exactly what jams the extraction pipeline, and a second Rick walks out asking the driver the wrong question.
- **Convergence 2:** the domestic truth is what *survives* — the one thing that stops anything is a kid asking for a sandwich and a van driver who is paid by the head.

### HARD RULES OF THE WORLD
- Extraction card locks the driver to the shuttle (can be outblanked, not fired).
- A replacement requires a signed shift sheet; a *process* request requires a scanned ID; mock-ups need signed waivers per sim run.
- The clipboard spells the catchphrase **"twenny minutes"**, because the card rap needs one malleable, unexplained, short phrase the driver can mangle. Planted as a straight promise once, then repriced twice.

## 8. WHAT STILL NEEDS A HUMAN

Straight talk about the ceiling:

1. **The engine logic is mechanical, not felt.** The C-route stinger (a second Rick on the sidewalk holding the wrong question) is structurally airtight and emotionally inert until a human decides *why this specific man can't ask for what he wants.* Machines can build the loop; they cannot decide the flinch. My version of Rick's flinch ("I don't—" interrupted by a technical correction) is the *shape* of one. A writer picks the *content*.

2. **Joke surface area doesn't scale linearly.** The premise machinery and structure are writable now. Density — the third laugh in a 5-second shot, the specific insult that lands on the third try, the accidental rhyme the room overhears — is a writers'-room function. A model reliably produces the *first* and *second* laugh in a beat and rarely the one that's funny because of what a *different* writer said twenty minutes ago.

3. **Escalation's *taste* is a human problem.** "One pretzel on eight receipts" is a good escalation. The ninth is where a writer earns their pay — it has to feel inevitable *and* wrong, and models (including this one) drift toward more volume instead of better angle.

4. **The stinger is not the note.** I can *hold* the carousel reloading forever with no dialogue (shot 36) because that's craft. Deciding *what should be on the tray* — that's the room. Get it wrong and the tag becomes a prop gag again, which is exactly the failure we started with.

5. **The verification pass cut my own best-sounding doctrine.** I was wrong about "no lesson learned" as a universal law; the corrected version (no sentimental *reformation*, withheld most from Rick) is what I built to. This is the honest ceiling: I can build the machine and I can be corrected. A human writers' room supplies the thing that makes you correct *before* the audience leaves, which is the only place it actually matters.
